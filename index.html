Can you trust this claim?

Your case: a headline that sounds convincing

Newspapers, adverts and social posts throw numbers at you all day. Some are honest, some are careless, and a few are built to mislead. In this investigation you'll learn three checks that separate them — and you'll finish by writing a headline that fails all three on purpose.

  • 1 · Graph framing — the same numbers, two charts, two very different impressions.
  • 2 · Sampling — why who you asked can matter more than how many you asked.
  • 3 · Correlation — two things rise together. Does one cause the other?

About 10–15 minutes. You can retry anything as many times as you like, and nothing is scored against you. Your notes go into an evidence notebook that stays in this browser only (nothing is sent anywhere). Use Reset at any time to wipe it.

Every school, town, person and dataset here is fictional and was generated for this lesson. See the educator guide for how the data were built.

3 · Correlation: two things rise together

In the seaside town of Port Lumen (fictional), a local paper notices that in weeks when the kiosk sells more ice cream, the clinic sees more sunburn. Its headline: Ice cream linked to sunburn! Investigate the 52 weeks of data.

Self-review checklist for your justification
  • Did you mention what happened to the correlation within seasons?
  • Did you name a plausible third variable (temperature / season)?
  • Did you avoid saying the data “prove” anything?
  • Did you say what evidence would support a causal claim (e.g. an experiment, or the link surviving after accounting for temperature)?

Exemplar: “Across the whole year r ≈ 0.8, but within each season the correlation is much weaker. Warm weeks push both numbers up, so the association alone does not show that ice cream causes sunburn.”

Turn on “colour by season”, then “within each season”. If the relationship is much weaker inside each season than across the year, the seasons (temperature) were doing most of the work.

Next practice

Find one real chart in the news this week. Ask the three questions: Where does the axis start? Who was in the sample? Could a third thing explain the link? Note that a chart can pass all three and still be wrong — and fail one and still be honest. Scrutiny, not blanket distrust.
